When someone buys a phone, a speaker, or a pair of headphones online, they are buying more than a set of specifications. They are trusting that what arrives will match what was described.

For an independent electronics seller, that confidence is part of the work.

## Be specific about condition

New, open-box, refurbished, and used are different things. Avoid language that leaves the customer guessing.

Show the actual condition of the item where possible. If the product photographs are representative, say so and offer additional pictures before the purchase.

List the model, capacity, colour, network compatibility, and included accessories. Be clear about chargers, cables, and packaging rather than relying on assumptions.

## Make support easy to understand

Warranty language deserves the same attention as the product description.

- State whether a warranty exists and who provides it.
- Explain what the customer should do if an item arrives damaged.
- Give clear information about returns and exclusions.
- Avoid implying a manufacturer partnership unless it exists.

A useful policy is one a customer can understand before they need it.

## Explain the transaction

Tell the customer how payment and delivery are arranged, how their order is identified, and how they can reach the business with a question.

Do not promise that a payment is protected by escrow, insurance, or a guarantee unless the specific arrangement actually provides it.

> Confidence grows when the important details are easy to check.

## Let consistency do the work

A complete catalogue, accurate availability, and reliable responses matter more than a dramatic sales claim.

Review the five questions customers ask most often. Turn those answers into a product checklist, then use it for every new listing.

Trust is rarely built by a single feature. It comes from the details lining up, from the first look to the moment the product is in the customer's hands.
